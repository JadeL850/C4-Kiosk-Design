SUBJECT TO VIEW

Flock Safety CEO Garrett Langley uses this promise to sell surveillance as a form of protection. The premise is simple: if something can be identified and seen, it can do no harm. But when did we ask for them to assume we were the harm? Who are they to assume we are the danger? Who are they? Do we get to observe them?

This exhibition takes that contradiction to the screen and provides an opportunity to observe what observes us back. Using real-time facial expression recognition, the website turns the viewer’s face into an input, triggering visual consequences based on how the system classifies their expression.

How it works

Gaze & Iris Tracking

Cameras that follow where you look, and read the pattern inside your eye.

  • Gaze. Invisible near-infrared light is shone at the eye. A camera tracks the dark pupil against a bright reflection on the cornea (the “glint”); the gap between them gives where you are looking, to within about 1°.
  • Iris. The iris’s texture forms before birth and differs even between identical twins. Software unwraps the iris ring into a flat strip and encodes it as a 2,048-bit “IrisCode”.
  • Match. Two codes are compared bit by bit. If fewer than about 32% of the bits differ, the system calls it the same person.

Source: J. Daugman, “How Iris Recognition Works”, IEEE, 2004.

How it works

Gait Recognition

Identifying a person by the way they walk: from a distance, without seeing their face, and without asking.

  • Capture. Ordinary CCTV video. The body’s outline is cut out of each frame, or a pose model places points on the joints.
  • Signature. One full stride is averaged into a single “gait energy image”. Stride length, rhythm, arm swing and joint angles become numbers a neural network compares.
  • Limits. Accuracy drops with different shoes, clothing, a carried bag or a new camera angle. But it works on blurry, low-resolution footage where a face cannot be read.

Source: J. Han & B. Bhanu, “Individual Recognition Using Gait Energy Image”, IEEE, 2006.

How it works

Minutiae Template

Your fingerprint is not stored as a picture. It is stored as a map of points.

  • Ridges. Friction ridges, about half a millimetre apart, form before birth and stay the same for life.
  • Minutiae. The points where a ridge ends or splits in two. A good print has roughly 40–100 of them, each saved as just a position, an angle and a type.
  • Template. That list of points, only a few hundred bytes, is the “template”. Matching rotates two point maps to line them up and scores how many points agree.

Sources: Maltoni et al., Handbook of Fingerprint Recognition; ISO/IEC 19794-2.

How it works

Measured

Biometrics turn a body into numbers that can be stored, searched and compared.

  • Face. A neural network converts a face into an “embedding”: a list of a few hundred numbers. Two faces count as the same person if their numbers are close enough.
  • Search. One face can be checked against millions of stored faces in seconds. Some systems search billions of photos scraped from the web.
  • Threshold. Every “match” is a score that passed a cut-off someone chose: a trade-off between missing a person and accusing the wrong one.

Source: NIST Face Recognition Technology Evaluation.

How it works

Categorized

Classifiers sort a measured person into labels such as age, gender, race and emotion.

  • Training. Models learn from photos that people have labelled, then output a probability for each label. The labels are only as fair as the people and categories behind them.
  • Bias. In 2018, commercial gender classifiers got darker-skinned women wrong up to 34.7% of the time, and lighter-skinned men 0.8% of the time.
  • Emotion. “Emotion recognition” reads facial muscle movements. A 2019 review of more than 1,000 studies found that facial movements do not reliably reveal what a person feels.

Sources: Buolamwini & Gebru, “Gender Shades”, 2018; Barrett et al., Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2019.

How it works

Judged

Labels become predictions about who someone is and what they might do.

  • Prediction. Predictive policing tools learn from past arrest records, so they send officers back to neighbourhoods that are already heavily policed. More patrols produce more records: a feedback loop.
  • Risk scores. A 2016 study of the COMPAS risk tool found that Black defendants who did not reoffend were labelled high risk almost twice as often as white defendants (45% vs 23%).
  • False matches. US government tests found many face recognition systems made 10 to 100 times more false matches on some groups, including African and East Asian faces.

Sources: Lum & Isaac, Significance, 2016; ProPublica, “Machine Bias”, 2016; NIST IR 8280, 2019.
